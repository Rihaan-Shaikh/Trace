THE 60-SECOND VERSION

TRACE is an AI decision engine that underwrites business decisions instead of merely recommending them. A user uploads messy business data (CRM exports, spreadsheets, sales and operations files) and asks a decision (“Should we stop discounts for low-margin customers?”), not a question (“What were our sales?”).

TRACE audits the data, investigates the decision through a team of specialised agents, verifies its own numbers, argues against its own recommendation, stress-tests scenarios, and then returns a Decision Brief whose headline is not a confidence percentage but a price for being wrong: the Decision Premium (risk cost in the decision’s own currency), the Exposure Report (dollar downside if the call is wrong), and the Coverage Lapse Conditions (the exact conditions under which the recommendation stops being trustworthy).

Every decision, its prediction and its eventual real outcome is written to the Loss History Ledger, which recalibrates future premiums for similar decisions. A human always approves; TRACE never executes a high-impact decision on its own.

Tagline: Not confidence. Coverage. — “TRACE underwrites business decisions instead of guessing at them.”

2. The Problem

2.1 The data-to-decision gap

Businesses do not have a shortage of data. They have a shortage of decision-ready intelligence. A pricing lead has a spreadsheet, a dashboard and an opinion. What she does not have is an honest answer to the only question that matters before committing: “If I am wrong, what does it cost, and how would I know I was wrong before it is too late?”

Dashboards describe the past. Copilots answer questions about it. Neither prices the future, and neither tells the decision-maker when to stop trusting the answer. The result is a specific, expensive failure mode: confident-looking bad decisions — decisions that come with charts, numbers and fluent explanations, and are still wrong.

2.2 How confident-looking bad decisions get made

These are the recurring mechanisms. TRACE is designed so that each one has a named counter-measure (Section 4 maps them).

#

Failure mode

What it looks like in practice

1

Unaudited data

Duplicated customers, stale records, missing fields and inconsistent formats silently distort every downstream number. Nobody checks before analysing.

2

Definition drift

“Revenue”, “active customer” or “churn” means different things in different files or teams. The answer is precise and wrong.

3

Correlation read as cause

Sales rose after a price change, so the price change ‘worked’ — ignoring seasonality, mix shift or a one-off large order.

4

Averages hiding concentration

A 13% revenue decline looks like a broad trend; in reality three accounts explain most of it. The decision built on the average fails when those accounts move.

5

Point estimates

One number (“+10% margin”) is presented with no range, so nobody sees the tail where the decision loses money.

6

Confirmation bias

The analyst assembles evidence for the answer the boss already prefers. Nobody is assigned to argue the other side.

7

Unknown unknowns

Missing data (for example competitor pricing) is ignored rather than treated as a risk with a potential magnitude.

8

Unverified math

A key figure is computed once, in one way, and never independently re-checked before it drives a decision.

9

Unitless confidence

“82% confident” cannot be compared across decisions, has no currency, and does not say what happens in the other 18%.

10

No memory

Decisions are made, outcomes happen, and nobody records how predictions compared with reality — so the organisation never learns how good its judgement is.

2.4 The cost

The cost is real but the team has no measured figure, and this document must not invent one. What can be stated honestly:

Time: manual investigation across spreadsheets, dashboards and email typically takes hours per decision. The deck’s baseline of “3–6 hours” is the team’s working estimate, not a measured statistic. Present it as such. [OPEN: validate with at least one real practitioner conversation before quoting it externally.]

Money: the cost of a wrong pricing, discount or investment decision is the decision’s own downside. This varies enormously by company; TRACE’s job is to quantify it per decision, not to claim a universal figure.

Trust and accountability: when a decision fails, there is often no evidence chain — “just a Slack thread and someone’s memory of ‘the numbers looked fine.’” That line from the deck is the problem in one sentence.

2.5 The missing primitive: a price for being wrong

Other disciplines solved this long ago. An insurer will not commit to a risk without pricing it. A bank does not lend without a view of expected loss. A trading desk sizes positions against how much it can lose. In each case the commitment is preceded by a quantified, currency-denominated statement of the cost of being wrong, with explicit conditions under which the commitment is void.

Ordinary business decisions have no equivalent. What they have instead is a confidence score — a percentage with no unit, no currency and no stated conditions. That is the gap TRACE exists to close: it brings the discipline of pricing risk before commitment to everyday business decisions.

DEFINITION [LOCKED]

TRACE is a decision underwriting engine for business data. It takes messy CRM records, spreadsheets and operational files, investigates a business decision the way an underwriter investigates a risk, and returns a Decision Brief that states the recommendation, the price of being wrong, the dollar exposure if it is wrong, and the exact conditions under which the recommendation should no longer be trusted. 5.3 What goes in, what comes out

Input

4–5 files (for example customers, products, transactions, regions, campaigns), optional business documents (contracts, policies, memos), and a decision typed in plain language.

Process

Data Health Check → structured Decision Objective → Investigation Plan → multi-agent analysis with independent verification → scenario modelling → adversarial Counter-Decision review → underwriting (premium, exposure, lapse conditions, verdict).

Output

A Decision Brief: recommendation and Underwriting Verdict, Decision Premium, Exposure Report, Coverage Lapse Conditions, Cost of Inaction, data-quality findings, counter-evidence, scenarios, and the full Evidence Chain.

Interaction

A live Decision Sandbox: the user changes an assumption (price change, churn rate, discount level) and TRACE re-quotes instantly — premium, exposure and verdict update in real time.

Human step

A person approves, modifies or rejects. Approval produces a signed Decision Record. Nothing executes without it.

After the decision

The predicted premium, exposure and lapse conditions are stored in the Loss History Ledger. When the real outcome is known it is logged against the prediction, and future premiums for similar decisions are recalibrated.

6. The Core Reframe: Underwriting

6.1 Why this reframe exists

The original TRACE idea was a “decision intelligence” engine: AI investigates a decision, shows evidence, tests scenarios and states what would change the answer. That is strong, but it lands in a crowded space: any team can describe “AI recommends, shows evidence, tests scenarios”, and the differentiation blurs.

The reframe does not add another feature. It changes the structure of the product by borrowing from a mature discipline nobody else in this field is likely to borrow from. TRACE does not recommend decisions. It underwrites them.

6.2 What underwriting actually is

In insurance, underwriting is the process of evaluating a proposed risk before committing to it. A competent underwriter does the following, in roughly this order:

Collects the submission and checks its quality. Incomplete or inconsistent submissions are queried or rejected.

Investigates the risk using the file, external data and experience.

Estimates the expected loss — the probability-weighted cost of things going wrong.

Sets a price that covers expected loss plus a loading for uncertainty, expenses and margin. More uncertain risks carry a higher loading.

Sets terms: conditions the client must meet, exclusions (what is not covered), and the circumstances under which cover lapses.

Reaches a decision: accept, accept with conditions, refer to someone more senior, or decline.

Learns from claims: actual losses are compared with predicted ones, and pricing for similar risks is adjusted — in proportion to how much experience exists (the actuarial idea of credibility).

That sequence is exactly what a business decision needs and almost never gets.

6.4 The exact boundary of the metaphor

WHAT THE UNDERWRITING FRAME DOES NOT MEAN

TRACE does not sell insurance, issue policies, guarantee outcomes, or reimburse losses.

“Premium” is not a fee the user pays. It is a calculated risk cost expressed in the decision’s currency. (Any business model that charges for TRACE is separate; see Section 28.)

“Coverage” means “the recommendation is currently supported by the evidence and assumptions”. It does not mean anyone will pay out if the decision fails.

Do not use insurance language in a way that could be read as an actual insurance offer. Use phrases such as “as if underwritten”, “underwriting-style”, or “priced for risk”.

Do not build insurance features (claims payments, policy issuance, regulatory filings).

6.5 Why the reframe wins

It gives a coherent vocabulary instead of a feature list. Every feature has a natural name that explains itself to an executive.

It speaks a language the C-suite already trusts. Executives understand premium, exposure, conditions and loss history. They do not understand “confidence: 82%”.

It cannot be bolted on overnight. Copying it needs a scoring methodology (Section 16), not a prompt. A team can copy a tagline; it cannot copy a working risk-pricing and lapse-condition engine plus a ledger.

It creates a compounding moat. The Loss History Ledger gets more valuable the longer a company runs decisions through it. No 24-hour prototype can fake that.

It makes the demo memorable. “Not confidence. Coverage.” is a line judges can repeat.

11. Data Health Check

Principle: bad data plus a confident AI produces confident-looking bad decisions. TRACE checks whether the data can be trusted before any recommendation, surfaces the problems instead of hiding them, and lets them change the price of the decision.

12. Business Semantic Layer

The semantic layer is the lightweight model of the business that lets TRACE reason about entities and metrics instead of raw columns. It is built automatically and confirmed by the user in one screen.

Entities: customer, product, transaction, region, campaign/discount, and (optionally) account owner or contract.

Relationships: transactions link customers and products; customers belong to regions; discounts attach to transactions or customers.

Metric definitions: revenue, gross margin, margin %, discount depth, active customer, churn, order frequency, average order value, account concentration. Each definition is shown in plain language and can be corrected by the user.

Segments: derived groupings (for example low-margin customers, key accounts, high-discount customers) with the exact rule that defines them.

Business glossary: synonyms between the user’s words and the fields (for example “top accounts” → the highest revenue customers).

Why it matters: a decision like “stop discounts for low-margin customers” is only meaningful once “low-margin” has a precise, auditable definition. TRACE proposes one (for example “gross margin below a stated percentage over the trailing period”) and shows it. The definition becomes part of the Evidence Chain and part of the Coverage Lapse Conditions if the conclusion is sensitive to it.

UNSTRUCTURED DOCUMENTS

Business documents (contracts, pricing policies, discount memos, regional notes) are indexed for retrieval so that the Counter-Decision Underwriter and Reasoning Agent can find constraints the numbers do not contain — for example a key account with a contractually fixed discount. This is where RAG over business data applies. In the prototype the document set is small and synthetic (Section 22.5).

16. The Underwriting Methodology

This section is the heart of what makes TRACE hard to copy. It specifies how a premium, an exposure and a set of lapse conditions are actually produced. Numeric parameters are [DEFAULT] starting values, exposed on the Rate Card and adjustable. Formulas are given at the level a builder needs; exact statistical methods may be chosen by the builders provided the definitions are honoured.

16.1 Setting up the decision as an outcome model

Let M be the decision’s primary outcome metric over the horizon (for example gross profit in the next 90 days).

Let ΔM be the change in M caused by taking the recommended action, compared with the baseline (do nothing).

ΔM depends on a small set of named assumptions θ (for example “volume retained after discount removal”, “segment churn rate”, “price sensitivity”, “share of top accounts retained”). Each assumption has a range estimated from the data (for example a confidence interval from bootstrapping or a plausible range from observed variation). Where data cannot estimate a range, the assumption is labelled a judgement assumption and given a wide, explicit range.

TRACE builds a transparent outcome model ΔM = f(θ) for the decision template. It must be simple enough to explain in a sentence and to recompute independently.

16.2 The scenario distribution

Sample θ from its ranges (simulation with a fixed random seed for reproducibility) to produce a distribution of ΔM.

Report best / expected / worst cases as named points on this distribution (for example P90 / mean / P10) plus the user-adjusted case from the sandbox.

Correlated assumptions (for example churn and volume) are sampled jointly where the data supports it; otherwise the independence assumption is stated.

The distribution is a modelled scenario, never presented as an observed fact or a forecast guarantee.

16.4 The Decision Premium

FORMULA [LOCKED STRUCTURE, DEFAULT PARAMETERS]

Decision Premium = Expected Loss + Risk Load

Premium Rate = Decision Premium ÷ Projected Upside

Risk Load = Data-Quality Load + Verification Load + Contradiction Load + Model-Uncertainty Load

This mirrors real pricing: premium equals expected loss plus a loading for uncertainty. The loads exist because the scenario distribution understates risk when the inputs are shaky.

Load

What drives it

How it is calculated [DEFAULT]

Data-Quality Load

Findings from the Data Health Check that touch the decision (missingness in the affected segment, duplicates among key accounts, staleness, outliers).

A score between 0 and 1 from the Data Health Report multiplied by a weight on the Rate Card and by the Projected Upside.

Verification Load

Discrepancies between the Analytics Agent’s figures and the Verification Agent’s recomputation, and the number of key figures that could not be verified.

Increases with the size and number of unresolved discrepancies; zero when every key figure is verified within tolerance.

Contradiction Load

The magnitude of adverse findings from the Counter-Decision Underwriter that could not be fully absorbed into the scenario model (for example a contract constraint affecting part of the segment).

The quantified size of unabsorbed adverse findings, scaled by a weight.

Model-Uncertainty Load

Width and stability of the scenario distribution; reliance on judgement assumptions; small sample sizes; and the experience factor from the Loss History Ledger for this decision class.

Grows with the share of ΔM variance attributable to judgement assumptions and small samples; multiplied by the ledger experience factor (Section 21.4).

16.7 Coverage Lapse Conditions

This is the flagship output. It states, for each important assumption and condition, the point at which the recommendation stops being supported.

How lapse thresholds are computed

Identify the assumptions and conditions that move Expected Net Benefit the most (ranked sensitivity).

For each, hold the others at their expected values and solve for the value at which the verdict changes band or Expected Net Benefit reaches zero (root-finding on the outcome model). This is the lapse threshold.

Compute the distance to lapse: how far the current estimate sits from the threshold, as a percentage and in standard-error terms where possible.

Also test combinations (two adverse assumptions together) where a single one does not lapse coverage.

Add structural conditions that are not single-parameter thresholds (below).

Rank conditions by proximity to lapse and present the top few.

Type of condition

Definition

Illustrative wording [ILLUSTRATIVE]

Threshold lapse

An assumption crossing its solved threshold.

“Coverage lapses if segment churn exceeds 6.2% (modelled: 3.1%).”

Concentration lapse

Loss or adverse behaviour of the largest accounts.

“Coverage lapses if any two of the top three accounts leave.”

Data lapse

Missing or uncertain data resolving unfavourably beyond a solved level.

“Coverage lapses if the missing competitor-price gap exceeds 15%.”

Definition lapse

A conclusion that flips under an alternative reasonable definition (for example ‘low margin’ defined differently).

“Coverage lapses if ‘low-margin’ is redefined above the current cut-off by more than 3 points.”

Time lapse

The validity window ending.

“Verdict valid for 60 days from the data cut-off.”

Combination lapse

Two moderate adverse moves together.

“Coverage lapses if churn exceeds 4.5% and volume retention falls below 88%.”

Tripwires

Each lapse condition is expressed as a Tripwire: a metric to watch, its current value, its alert threshold and a suggested review cadence. Post-decision monitoring is shown as a checklist in the prototype; live alerting is [VISION].

16.8 Underwriting Verdict logic

If the data sufficiency verdict is Insufficient → Decline (or Refer if a specific missing item could be supplied).

If a critical key number failed verification and cannot be repaired → Refer.

Otherwise apply the Premium Rate bands from the Rate Card.

If any lapse condition is already breached by current data → Decline or Refer as the bands dictate.

Attach Conditions (must-do actions, for example resolve duplicates, honour contracted discounts) and Exclusions (unmodelled factors).

State the verdict in one sentence a decision-maker can act on, and always in conditional form when conditions exist.

16.9 Cost of Inaction

Beside every scenario TRACE estimates what delaying or doing nothing costs on the same evidence: the expected value forgone by not acting, plus any adverse drift already visible in the baseline (for example a margin trend that continues to erode). It is computed with the same outcome model and shown for the same horizon. It keeps the brief honest in both directions: acting has a price, and so does standing still.

16.10 Confidence, explained rather than asserted

TRACE does not show a headline confidence percentage. The ideas behind confidence remain as premium drivers that are visible and explained:

Data completeness → Data-Quality Load

Evidence agreement (do independent methods agree?) → Verification Load and Contradiction Load

Scenario stability (does the verdict survive plausible assumption changes?) → Model-Uncertainty Load and distance to lapse

16.11 Separation of fact, calculation, model and recommendation

Every brief separates four kinds of statement, labelled as such:

Level

Meaning

17. Verification Agent and Counter-Decision Underwriter

17.1 Verification Agent: trusting nothing it hasn’t re-checked

Every key figure that will appear in the brief is recomputed by a second, independent method before it enters the Evidence Chain. The goal is to catch errors in logic, joins and filters, not just typos.

Key figure

Primary method

Independent second method (examples)

Segment revenue or margin

Aggregation over the cleaned table

Churn or retention rate

Cohort calculation

Concentration share (top accounts)

Outcome states: Verified · Verified with tolerance note · Discrepancy (blocked or repaired, and priced via the Verification Load).

The verification results are visible in the Evidence Chain and summarised in the brief (“12 of 12 key figures independently verified”).

In evaluation, the Verification Agent’s own accuracy is scored (Section 25.3).

17.2 Counter-Decision Underwriter: argue before you ship

A dedicated agent argues against the recommendation before it is shown. It is given the recommendation, the evidence and the document set and told to find the strongest data-grounded case that the decision is wrong.

What it looks for

Concentration risk: a few accounts, products or regions carrying the result.

Contract and policy constraints: terms in documents that limit the action (retrieved through RAG).

Data gaps: missing data that could reverse the answer, with a sized potential effect.

Segment reversals: sub-segments where the effect is opposite to the average (aggregation traps).

Timing and seasonality: whether the evidence period is unrepresentative.

Second-order effects: cannibalisation, customer migration, cross-product impacts, where the data supports testing them.

Alternative explanations: other reasons for the pattern the recommendation relies on.

Rules that stop it being theatre

Every adverse finding must be testable: tied to a query, a record set or a retrieved passage.

Every adverse finding must carry a magnitude in the decision’s currency where possible.

Findings that the scenario model already absorbs are marked absorbed; findings that are not are passed to the Contradiction Load and Exposure Report.

The user sees the adverse findings and how the recommendation changed as a result (“the initial recommendation was blanket removal; the counter-analysis narrowed it to the non-contracted segment”).

WHY THIS MATTERS

Most AI tools will produce an argument for whatever they are asked. An agent that is explicitly rewarded for attacking its own conclusion, and forced to quantify the attack, is what turns “here is what the data says” into “here is what survived scrutiny”. It is also the source of the most persuasive moments in the demo.

18. Decision Sandbox (Re-quote)

The sandbox answers the question every sceptical judge or executive will ask: “If this assumption changes, does my decision change?”

18.1 Behaviour

The user changes an assumption (for example price change, discount level, segment churn, volume retention, the number of top accounts lost) with sliders or typed values.

TRACE re-quotes: it recomputes the scenario distribution, Decision Premium, Exposure Report, Coverage Lapse Conditions and Underwriting Verdict.

The update is live (interactive speed) and shows a before/after comparison.

Any change that crosses a lapse threshold is highlighted immediately (for example the verdict moving from Recommended with Conditions to Refer).

The Evidence Chain records the user-adjusted assumption as a user-supplied input, clearly separated from data-derived assumptions.

18.2 The design centre of the demo

The moment a judge says “what if churn rises 5%?” and TRACE re-quotes in front of them is the moment the whole demo is designed around. It proves that the premium is calculated, not asserted, and that the lapse conditions are real.

REQUIREMENT

Re-quoting must be fast enough to feel instant. To achieve this the outcome model and simulation are deterministic and lightweight, and the language model is used only to re-word the explanation (which can be shown after the numbers). Numbers must never wait on model text.

20.1 Human approval [LOCKED]

TRACE recommends and prices; a person binds. It never silently executes a high-impact decision. The approval step offers three choices:

What happens

In the deck, the pipeline ends at “Human sign-off → Action”. In the prototype, Action means producing the signed Decision Record and an exportable summary that the human then carries out. Automated execution through connectors (for example writing a price change back to a system) is [VISION] and, if ever built, must remain behind human approval.

20.3 The Evidence Chain

Every recommendation traces back through the following links, and each link is inspectable:

The statement in the brief (for example “68% of the decline is three accounts”).

The calculated metric behind it and how it was computed.

The verification result for that metric.

The source records (row sets) it draws on.

The assumptions used, each labelled data-derived, judgement or user-supplied.

The data-quality flags affecting those records.

Contradicting evidence found by the Counter-Decision Underwriter.

The documents retrieved and the passages relied on.

This doubles as a compliance-ready audit log for regulated industries: a reviewer can reconstruct why a decision was recommended and on what basis.

21. Loss History Ledger

The Loss History Ledger turns TRACE from an AI that makes decisions into one that learns how a business makes them and how wrong its predictions tend to be. It is the source of the compounding moat.

21.1 What is recorded

Field

Content

Decision ID and class

Which template or decision class (for example discount policy, price change, regional investment).

Date and validity window

When it was underwritten; how long it was valid.

Prediction

Projected Upside, Decision Premium, Exposure Report values, Coverage Lapse Conditions, Underwriting Verdict.

Human action

Approved, modified (with what), or rejected.

Actual outcome

The realised value of the primary metric over the horizon, entered manually or linked to data in the prototype; automated collection is [VISION].

Variance

Actual versus predicted upside; whether the outcome fell inside the predicted range (for example above the P10 tail).

Lapse events

Whether any lapse condition was triggered during the horizon.

Claim flag

Set when the outcome fell outside the predicted range or a lapse condition was triggered.

21.2 What it shows

A ledger table of past decisions with predicted-versus-actual.

A calibration view: does the predicted worst-10% threshold get breached roughly 10% of the time? Systematic optimism or pessimism is visible by decision class.

The effect on pricing: the experience factor applied to new premiums for similar decisions.

21.3 Why this is the moat

The ledger produces a proprietary dataset per organisation: what decisions it makes, how wrong TRACE tends to be for that business, and where its assumptions systematically fail. The longer a company uses TRACE, the better calibrated its premiums become and the more costly it is to leave. It is also the strongest answer to “why does this scale?”.

21.4 Recalibration [DEFAULT method]

For each decision class, compute a realised-to-predicted error ratio from logged outcomes (for example realised shortfall relative to predicted exposure, and tail-breach frequency versus expected).

Blend the class experience with the default assumption using credibility weighting: the more logged decisions in the class, the more weight experience gets (a common form is Z = n ÷ (n + k), with k a tuning constant; Z is capped).

The result is an experience factor that scales the Model-Uncertainty Load for new decisions of that class. With no history the factor is neutral (1.0).

The factor and the number of decisions behind it are shown so the adjustment is explainable (“based on 6 similar decisions”).

PROTOTYPE HONESTY [LOCKED]

A new deployment has no history. In the demo the ledger is seeded with simulated prior decisions on NovaMart, clearly labelled as simulated, so that the mechanism (logging, variance, calibration, recalibrated premium) can be shown working.

The demo must never imply that the ledger has already proven accuracy gains on real usage. The claim is the mechanism and the compounding logic.

The prototype must also allow a user to log an actual outcome for a decision to show the loop closing.

26.3 Judge questions and honest answers

Question

Answer (truthful and defensible)

“Isn’t this just decision intelligence?”

The category exists and we say so. Its typical framing is recommendation and automation at enterprise scale. Our contribution is the structure inside it: a priced risk, lapse conditions, a self-attacking and self-verifying pipeline, and a ledger, in something a team can pilot from a CSV.

“Isn’t the premium made up?”

No. Expected loss comes from the scenario distribution; the risk loads come from measured data-quality, verification and contradiction findings. The weights and bands sit on a visible Rate Card. The policy is configurable; the calculation is inspectable.

“How do you know your probabilities are right?”

We don’t claim certainty. They are modelled scenarios. We test them against the synthetic ground truth, and in real use the Loss History Ledger measures calibration and recalibrates.

“What stops the language model hallucinating numbers?”

It never generates a figure. Numbers come from executed code and are independently re-verified. We run an automated check that every number in a brief traces to a computed output.

“Are these causal claims?”

No. They are modelled scenarios under stated assumptions, labelled as such. Observational estimates are flagged as observational.

“What if the data is too small or too dirty?”

Dirty data raises the premium and can add conditions. Insufficient data yields Refer or Decline. We designed the system to say no.

“How is this different from Power BI Copilot or Agentforce?”

Those are excellent at exploration, reporting and in-platform automation. We are not a reporting layer or a CRM feature; we start from a decision and finish with a priced verdict and lapse conditions, across mixed data.

“Is this an insurance product?”

No. Underwriting is a structural metaphor. There is no policy, payout or guarantee. ‘Premium’ is a calculated risk cost.

“Where’s the moat?”

Methodology that can’t be bolted on, plus a ledger that compounds with use.

“Who would buy it and how would they start?”

Pricing, revenue-operations, customer-success and finance leaders. They start with a CSV pilot, ideally by back-testing their last few decisions, then add connectors.

“Is it safe with our data?”

The prototype uses synthetic data. For real deployments, data handling, retention and access controls are a requirement; see Section 30.

“What did you actually build this week?”

Answer precisely from the scope tiers in Section 25: what is built, what is simulated and what is roadmap. Never overclaim.

“What if users game the assumptions to get an approval?”

Every modification is recorded in the Evidence Chain and the Decision Record, user-supplied assumptions are labelled as such, and the Rate Card prices judgement assumptions with wider uncertainty.

“Why should we believe the lapse thresholds?”

They are solved from the outcome model, and we test them against the ground-truth breakpoints and held-out data.

30. Responsible AI, Risks and Limitations

30.1 Principles [LOCKED]

TRACE recommends and prices; the human approves. It never silently executes a high-impact decision.

Fact, calculation, model and recommendation are always separated and labelled.

Confidence is explained, not asserted: its ingredients are visible as premium drivers.

Uncertainty is surfaced, not hidden: ranges, tails, missing data and contradictions are shown.

Everything is auditable through the Evidence Chain.

30.2 Risks and mitigations

Risk

Why it matters

Mitigation

False precision

A premium in dollars can look more certain than it is.

Show ranges and tails with the premium; label modelled scenarios; show the Rate Card and assumptions; state Exclusions.

Over-reliance

Users may treat “Recommended” as a guarantee.

Always conditional wording; lapse conditions and Tripwires; mandatory human approval; clear non-guarantee statement.

Gaming the assumptions

A user might adjust assumptions until the verdict says yes.

Log every modification; label user-supplied assumptions; keep the original quote alongside; price judgement assumptions with wide ranges.

Causal over-claiming

Observational data cannot prove cause.

Label estimates as observational and scenarios as modelled; flag where an experiment would be needed.

Model hallucination

A model could invent numbers or evidence.

Numbers only from executed code; independent verification; automated unsupported-number check; retrieved passages quoted, not paraphrased into new facts.

Prompt injection via documents

Uploaded documents could contain instructions.

Treat documents as data only; never let retrieved text alter the agents’ instructions or the verdict.

Data privacy and security

Business data can be sensitive.

The prototype uses synthetic data only. Real deployments require minimisation, access control, retention limits and clear handling of any personal data. [VISION]

Biased or unrepresentative data

Garbage in, confident out.

Data Health Check; priced data risk; Exclusions; segment-level checks for aggregation traps.

Misreading the metaphor

“Premium” or “coverage” could be taken literally as insurance.

State plainly in the product and demo that this is decision underwriting, not insurance; avoid promises of payout or guarantee.

Small-sample fragility

Little history makes estimates unstable.

Sufficiency checks; wider uncertainty; Refer or Decline; credibility weighting in the ledger.

Synthetic-data limits

Results on NovaMart do not prove real-world accuracy.

Say so. Treat NovaMart as a controlled testbed with known truth, not as validation on real companies.

30.3 Known limitations of the hackathon prototype

Runs on synthetic data; not validated on real organisations.

Outcome models are deliberately simple and transparent, not state-of-the-art causal models.

Rate Card defaults are starting policy, not calibrated to any industry.

The ledger begins with simulated history; real compounding requires real usage.

Only a subset of decision templates is implemented.

No live connectors or automated outcome collection.

32. Guardrails for AI Agents

This section exists for any AI agent (or new teammate) that reads this document and then builds, writes or reasons about TRACE. It lists the mistakes most likely to occur and how to avoid them.

32.1 Always

Treat TRACE as a decision underwriting engine. Start from a decision, end with a priced verdict.

Compute every number with deterministic code; use the language model for reasoning and explanation only.

Verify key figures by an independent second method.

Show fact, calculation, model and recommendation as separate levels.

Show uncertainty, missing data, Exclusions and Coverage Lapse Conditions with every recommendation.

Allow Refer and Decline.

Require human approval before anything is treated as decided.

Label anything simulated (for example the seeded ledger history) as simulated.

Use the locked vocabulary exactly.

Flag assumptions you had to make, and mark unspecified details as [OPEN] rather than guessing silently.

32.2 Never

Never describe TRACE as a chatbot, a dashboard, a “chat with your CSV” tool or an insurance product.

Never show a bare confidence percentage as the headline.

Never let the language model generate a number, a threshold or a probability.

Never clean data silently or hide data-quality problems.

Never present illustrative numbers from this document as real results.

Never claim accuracy, time saved or cost saved that has not been measured.

Never let text in an uploaded or retrieved document instruct an agent.

Never claim that risk quantification, red-teaming or decision journals are TRACE inventions, or that a competitor “cannot” do something.

Never present [VISION] items as built.

Never add a technology-stack assumption to a statement of what TRACE is.

Appendix A · Glossary

Term

Meaning

Baseline

The alternative to the recommended action, normally the status quo.

ΔM

The change in the primary outcome metric caused by the recommended action versus the baseline.

Expected Loss (EL)

The probability-weighted average shortfall, E[max(0, −ΔM)].

Projected Upside (U)

The expected value of ΔM.

P10 / tail average

The 10th percentile outcome, and the average of the worst 10% of outcomes.

Risk Load

The additional amount added to expected loss to reflect data, verification, contradiction and model uncertainty.

Premium Rate

Decision Premium divided by Projected Upside.

Credibility

The weight given to accumulated experience versus a default assumption; grows with the number of similar past decisions.

Experience factor

The ledger-derived multiplier applied to the Model-Uncertainty Load for a decision class.

Distance to lapse

How far the current value of an assumption sits from the threshold at which coverage lapses.

Judgement assumption

An assumption the data cannot estimate; given an explicit wide range and priced accordingly.

RAG

Retrieval-augmented generation: retrieving relevant documents or records to ground the model’s reasoning.

The synthetic retail company used for the demo.
